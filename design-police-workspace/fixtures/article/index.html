The Quiet Case for Boring Infrastructure

Every team eventually meets the moment where the interesting choice and the correct choice point in opposite directions. The interesting choice has a conference talk attached to it. The correct choice has a decade of uneventful uptime attached to it, which is a much harder thing to put on a slide, and so it tends to lose the argument in rooms where the loudest person has recently read a blog post.

What boring actually buys you

Boring buys you the ability to be woken at three in the morning and still understand the system you are looking at. It buys you a hiring pool that does not need a six-week onboarding to make a one-line change. It buys you the option of leaving the thing alone for two years, which is the single most underrated feature any piece of infrastructure can offer, and the one nobody writes benchmarks for.

The cost nobody prices in

The cost of novelty is not the learning curve. The learning curve is fine; engineers like learning curves. The cost is that every novel component is a component whose failure modes are not yet folklore, and folklore is the only documentation that survives a team turning over twice.
